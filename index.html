Cancers of Reproductive Organs

Cancer happens when cells grow out of control, ignoring the normal signals that regulate division and apoptosis. Four reproductive organ cancers are covered below, each with where it occurs, how it is detected, and how it is treated.

Prostate Cancer

Male reproductive system
Where it occurs

The prostate sits below the bladder and surrounds the urethra. Tumors form in its glandular tissue and can compress the urethra as they grow.

Detection
  • PSA blood test
  • Digital rectal exam (DRE)
  • MRI or ultrasound imaging
  • Biopsy to confirm malignancy
Treatment
  • Active surveillance for slow-growing cases
  • Surgery (prostatectomy)
  • Radiation or hormone therapy
  • Chemotherapy for advanced stages

Ovarian Cancer

Female reproductive system
Where it occurs

Ovarian cancer begins in the ovaries, which produce eggs and hormones. It is one of the most lethal gynecologic cancers because early symptoms are vague and often missed.

Detection
  • No reliable early-detection test exists
  • CA-125 blood test (not definitive)
  • Transvaginal ultrasound
  • Biopsy or surgical exploration to confirm
Treatment
  • Surgery to remove ovaries, uterus, and affected tissue
  • Chemotherapy after surgery
  • Targeted therapy for BRCA mutation types

Cervical Cancer

Female reproductive system
Where it occurs

Cervical cancer starts in the cervix, the narrow lower part of the uterus. Nearly all cases are caused by HPV, making it one of the most preventable cancers due to vaccines and screening.

Detection
  • Pap smear to detect abnormal cells
  • HPV DNA test
  • Colposcopy and biopsy if needed
  • Routine screening catches most cases early
Treatment
  • Cone biopsy or LEEP for early precancerous cells
  • Hysterectomy for localized cancer
  • Radiation and chemotherapy for advanced stages
  • HPV vaccination (Gardasil) prevents the main cancer-causing strains

Breast Cancer

Breast tissue
Where it occurs

Breast cancer develops in the breast's ducts or lobules and is the most common cancer in women. It can spread to lymph nodes first, then to other organs.

Detection
  • Mammogram, recommended regularly after age 40
  • Breast self-examination and clinical exam
  • Ultrasound or MRI for high-risk patients
  • Core needle biopsy to confirm
Treatment
  • Lumpectomy or mastectomy
  • Radiation or chemotherapy
  • Hormone therapy for hormone-receptor-positive types
  • Targeted therapy (e.g., Herceptin for HER2-positive)

Diabetes Types I, II, and Gestational

Diabetes is a group of diseases affecting how the body uses glucose. All types involve problems with insulin, the hormone that lets cells absorb glucose for energy.

Distinguishing the Three Types

Type Cause Onset Insulin Status Management
Type I Autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing beta cells Usually childhood or young adulthood Little to no insulin produced Daily insulin injections or pump
Type II Cells become resistant to insulin Usually adults, increasing in younger people Insulin produced but not used effectively Diet, exercise, metformin, sometimes insulin
Gestational Pregnancy hormones cause temporary insulin resistance During pregnancy, usually second trimester Insulin produced but blocked by hormones Diet, exercise, sometimes insulin; resolves after birth

Risk Factors

  • Type I: Family history and genetic markers are the main risk factors. Environmental triggers like certain viral infections may also play a role.
  • Type II: Obesity, physical inactivity, family history, age over 45, high blood pressure, and certain ethnic backgrounds all raise risk.
  • Gestational: Obesity before pregnancy, previous gestational diabetes, and family history of Type II all increase risk. Having it also raises lifetime risk of developing Type II.

How Diabetes Affects the Body

Eyes — Retinopathy

High blood sugar damages tiny blood vessels in the retina. They can leak or grow abnormally, eventually causing vision loss. A leading cause of adult blindness.

Kidneys — Nephropathy

Diabetes damages the kidney's dense network of small blood vessels over time. Left untreated, this leads to kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant.

Arteries — Atherosclerosis

High glucose accelerates fatty plaque buildup inside artery walls. People with diabetes are two to four times more likely to develop heart disease.

Nerves — Neuropathy

Damaged vessels starve nerves of oxygen. The result is numbness or burning pain, usually in the feet first. Severe cases can lead to infections requiring amputation.

Science Information Sources

Primary vs. Secondary Sources

Primary Source

Original research reported by the scientists who conducted it. Includes peer-reviewed articles, preprints, and clinical trial reports.

  • Raw data and full methodology included
  • No interpretation layer between you and the findings
  • Can be dense and technical
Secondary Source

An interpretation or summary of primary sources written by someone other than the researchers. Includes news articles, review papers, and textbooks.

  • Written in accessible language for a broad audience
  • Synthesizes multiple studies at once
  • Always filtered through the writer's framing

How to Scan a Scientific Paper

  1. Abstract first. A 150-250 word summary of the whole paper. If it is not relevant, stop here.
  2. Introduction. Background and motivation. Explains what problem they are solving.
  3. Figures and tables. This is where the actual data lives. Reading just the captions can give you the key findings.
  4. Discussion and conclusion. How the authors interpret their results and what limitations they acknowledge.
  5. Methods (if needed). Only dig in here if you need to evaluate whether the study was conducted properly.

Quality Secondary Sources

mayoclinic.org

Written and reviewed by medical professionals. Clear, thorough, and regularly updated. Good for understanding diseases and treatments.

U.S. Centers for Disease Control. Tracks disease data and publishes public health guidelines based on current evidence.
